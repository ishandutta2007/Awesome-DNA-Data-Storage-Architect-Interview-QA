# Topic 10: Cross-Functional Collaboration

## Overview
Working effectively with molecular biologists/wet-lab teams, computer scientists/information theorists, and systems/infrastructure engineers as a DNA Data Storage Architect.

---

### Q1: How do you build effective collaborative relationships with molecular biology/wet-lab teams, given that a DNA Data Storage Architect's background is often more heavily weighted toward information theory/computer science than hands-on molecular biology expertise?

**A:**
**Trust-building and collaboration approach:**
1. **Invest genuine effort in understanding the practical realities and constraints of DNA synthesis/sequencing wet-lab workflows, not just their abstracted computational/information-theoretic representation:** An architect who understands only the abstracted error-rate/throughput parameters of synthesis and sequencing (Topics 05-06) without genuine appreciation for the practical wet-lab realities generating those parameters (sample handling, reagent/protocol variability, the genuine difficulty of achieving reproducible, well-controlled experimental conditions at scale) risks designing systems with unrealistic assumptions or overlooking practically important considerations that wet-lab collaborators would immediately recognize — building genuine technical literacy in the underlying molecular biology (even without personally executing wet-lab protocols) substantially improves collaborative credibility and design quality
2. **Treat empirical wet-lab characterization data as authoritative over theoretical/literature-derived assumptions, consistent with the empirical-validation emphasis throughout this repository:** As emphasized in Topics 03, 05, and 06, effective DNA storage system design requires empirically-grounded channel/error characterization specific to the actual system's real components and conditions — an architect should actively seek out and prioritize wet-lab collaborators' direct experimental characterization data over generic literature figures, and should genuinely partner with wet-lab teams in designing the specific characterization experiments needed (analogous to the optimal experimental design collaboration principles discussed in the Organ-on-a-Chip Simulator repository) rather than requesting characterization data in a purely one-directional, extractive relationship
3. **Communicate encoding/error-correction design constraints and rationale in terms wet-lab collaborators can directly engage with and provide informed feedback on:** Translating information-theoretic design considerations (e.g., "we need to minimize homopolymer runs above length 4 to keep the synthesis error rate below X threshold") into concrete, wet-lab-relevant terms enables genuine two-way technical dialogue rather than wet-lab collaborators being asked to simply execute specifications handed down without adequate context for them to contribute their own relevant expertise and catch potential issues early

### Q2: Describe how you would collaborate with information theorists/coding theory specialists and broader computer science/systems engineering colleagues to ensure DNA storage system design appropriately integrates both the coding-theoretic and systems-engineering dimensions of the problem.

**A:**
**Collaborative practices:**
1. **Ensure coding theory specialists have accurate, current empirical channel characterization to design against, not idealized theoretical channel models alone:** As emphasized in Topic 03, effective error-correcting code design for the actual DNA storage channel requires accurate empirical characterization of the system's actual, specific error profile — the architect should ensure close collaboration between coding theory specialists and the molecular biology/wet-lab characterization effort (Q1), providing coding theorists with the concrete, empirically-grounded channel model inputs their theoretical code design work requires, rather than allowing coding theory design work to proceed based on generic or idealized channel assumptions disconnected from the system's actual real-world performance
2. **Bridge coding-theoretic optimality with practical systems engineering constraints (computational complexity, latency, implementation practicality):** A coding scheme that is theoretically near-optimal in terms of achieved information density/error-correction capability may nonetheless be impractical if its encoding/decoding computational complexity is prohibitive for the system's actual operational requirements (e.g., real-time or near-real-time decoding needs) — the architect should facilitate genuine dialogue between coding theory specialists (who may naturally optimize primarily for coding-theoretic performance metrics) and systems engineering colleagues (who understand the practical computational/latency constraints the deployed system must satisfy), ensuring the ultimately selected coding scheme represents a genuinely well-reasoned joint optimization across both dimensions rather than a theoretically elegant solution that proves impractical to actually implement and operate
3. **Maintain a systems-level view integrating encoding, error correction, synthesis/sequencing technology, and storage/retrieval architecture as a coherently co-designed whole, actively counteracting the natural tendency for different specialist collaborators to locally optimize their own component in isolation:** Given the genuinely cross-cutting nature of many DNA storage design decisions (e.g., Topic 05's discussion of how synthesis technology choice affects encoding scheme design, which affects error-correction requirements, which affects sequencing coverage depth requirements, Topic 06), the architect's core distinctive value is maintaining and actively defending this integrated systems-level view — proactively identifying and facilitating resolution of cross-component design tensions that might otherwise go unaddressed if each specialist collaborator (coding theorist, molecular biologist, systems engineer) worked in relative isolation optimizing only their own component against potentially inconsistent or incomplete assumptions about the other components

## Summary
Effective cross-functional collaboration requires the DNA Data Storage Architect to build genuine technical literacy across both molecular biology and information theory/systems engineering domains, prioritize empirical characterization over theoretical assumptions, and actively maintain the integrated, cross-cutting systems-level view that prevents specialist collaborators from locally optimizing their own component in ways that produce an incoherent or impractical overall system design.
